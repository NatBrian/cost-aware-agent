# Category 2: Agent Stopping, Early Exit & Adaptive Termination

Papers about teaching agents when to stop reasoning, early exit from computation, or adaptive termination. These papers establish the empirical and theoretical foundations for why stopping matters — and provide initial methods for learning termination behavior.

---

### CaRT: Teaching LLM Agents to Know When They Know Enough

- **Authors:** Grace Liu, Yuxiao Qu, Jeff Schneider, Aarti Singh, Aviral Kumar
- **Year/Venue:** 2025, arXiv preprint (Carnegie Mellon University)
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.08517

**Background:** Many tasks require models to strategically gather information over multiple rounds before acting. Strategic information gathering requires knowing not only how to acquire information, but also when to stop gathering and make a decision. LLMs typically fail to recognize the best points to stop — often either overshooting or undershooting.

**Motivation:** Off-the-shelf LLMs struggle to accurately predict their probability of success and are unable to conduct principled exploration. The ability to recognize when "I know enough" is essential to efficient and reliable problem solving. Simply training models with length penalties is brittle and doesn't generalize.

**Approach:** CaRT (Counterfactuals and Reasoning for Termination) fine-tunes LLMs using **counterfactual pairs** of trajectories: one where termination is appropriate and a minimally modified version where it is not. The approach has two components: **(1) Hard Negative Counterfactuals:** For each trajectory where terminating is correct (leads to high answer success), CaRT constructs a hard negative where termination would be suboptimal, ensuring the only difference is the presence or absence of genuinely necessary information. This isolates the true causal signal for termination. In interactive settings, the final question-answer pair is perturbed; in non-interactive settings, a sequence of reasoning steps is modified. **(2) Verbal Reasoning:** Training examples are augmented with explicit reasoning traces (generated by GPT-4o) explaining why the current state warrants termination or continuation. These reasoning traces serve as an implicit value function, helping the model predict the best action by reasoning about potential implications. The model is trained via SFT on these counterfactual+reasoning examples, with optional additional RL (GRPO) using a binary reward for correct termination/continuation decisions.

**Training Method:** SFT on counterfactual examples with GPT-4o-generated reasoning traces. Optional GRPO-based RL with binary reward (+1 for correct termination/continuation, -1 otherwise).

**Key Results:** CaRT improves efficiency of information gathering and task success rate compared to SFT baselines in both interactive medical diagnosis and math problem solving. The counterfactual pairs prevent learning of spurious correlations. The verbal reasoning traces improve generalization.

**Weakness:** GDP-4o-generated reasoning traces may introduce biases. The counterfactual construction process is domain-specific. Only tested on two domains. Binary termination/continue actions — no cost-sensitivity.

**Relevance to Our Work:** **Highest.** CaRT is the closest prior work — it explicitly trains models to terminate information gathering. Our proposal extends CaRT by: (a) using a separate monitor agent instead of self-termination, (b) incorporating cost-awareness (not just correctness), (c) using full RL training of the monitor (not just SFT on counterfactuals), and (d) producing a continuous stopping signal rather than a binary decision.

### Dynamic Early Exit in Reasoning Models

- **Authors:** (Multiple authors, 2025)
- **Year/Venue:** 2025, arXiv preprint
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.15895

**Background:** Large reasoning models (LRMs) generate extensive chain-of-thought, but the reasoning pattern exhibits a natural structure: initial exploration followed by convergence. Current models generate the full thinking process regardless of whether convergence has been reached.

**Motivation:** Can we detect when the reasoning model has converged on an answer and exit early, saving computation without sacrificing accuracy?

**Approach:** Dynamic Early Exit exploits the **generation pattern of large reasoning models** — analysis reveals that reasoning tokens follow a characteristic trajectory: high entropy during exploration, decreasing entropy as the model converges on a solution, and low entropy near the final answer. The method: **(1) Convergence Detection:** Monitors token-level probability metrics (entropy, max probability, probability gap between top two tokens) during generation to detect when the model has "settled" on an answer. When entropy drops below a threshold and remains low for a sustained window, the model is deemed to have converged. **(2) Branch-Parallel Decoding:** To avoid the latency penalty of sequential early-exit checking, uses speculative parallel decoding — multiple possible continuations are explored in parallel, and the first to converge is selected. This enables early exit without sacrificing throughput.

**Training Method:** No model training. Convergence detection is based on statistical analysis of token probabilities. Branch-parallel decoding is an inference-time acceleration technique.

**Key Results:** Significant token reduction on math reasoning benchmarks without accuracy loss. The convergence detection reliably identifies when further reasoning is unnecessary. Branch-parallel decoding enables early exit without latency penalty.

**Weakness:** The convergence threshold is a fixed hyperparameter. Token probability patterns may not generalize across model families. Only tested on math reasoning.

**Relevance to Our Work:** **Medium-High.** Dynamic Early Exit provides a signal-based approach to detecting when reasoning is "done" — similar to what our monitor agent would do. However, it uses simple statistical thresholds rather than a learned monitor. The entropy/confidence patterns could serve as features for our monitor agent.

### The Diminishing Returns of Early-Exit Decoding in Modern LLMs

- **Authors:** (Multiple authors, 2026)
- **Year/Venue:** 2026, arXiv preprint
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23701

**Background:** Early-exit methods — terminating computation at intermediate transformer layers when confidence is high — have been proposed as a way to reduce inference cost. These methods assume that deeper layers provide diminishing returns.

**Motivation:** As LLMs have grown larger and more capable, does early-exit decoding still provide benefits, or have modern LLMs become so well-trained that all layers contribute meaningfully?

**Approach:** A systematic empirical study of early-exit decoding across modern LLMs at various scales. The paper evaluates: (1) whether intermediate layers produce accurate enough predictions to justify early exit, (2) how the benefit of deeper layers varies with model scale, (3) whether confidence-based exit criteria are well-calibrated in modern LLMs. Key finding: in modern large LLMs, **early layers are surprisingly capable** — they often produce correct predictions with high confidence, but the exit criteria struggle to distinguish between cases where early exit would be safe vs. risky.

**Training Method:** No training — empirical evaluation of existing early-exit methods on modern LLMs.

**Key Results:** Early-exit methods show diminishing returns in modern LLMs — the accuracy gap between early and final layers is smaller than expected, but confidence-based exit criteria are poorly calibrated. The "diminishing returns" refer to the diminishing benefit of adding more layers, not diminishing returns of computation per se.

**Weakness:** Evaluation only — no new method proposed. Calibration issues may be model-specific.

**Relevance to Our Work:** **Medium.** This paper's finding that confidence-based exit criteria are poorly calibrated reinforces our argument that a learned monitor (rather than simple confidence thresholds) is needed for reliable stopping decisions.

### s1: Simple Test-Time Scaling

- **Authors:** Niklas Muennighoff, Zitong Yang, Weijia Shi, Xiang Lisa Li, Li Fei-Fei, Hannaneh Hajishirzi, Luke Zettlemoyer, Percy Liang, Emmanuel Candès, Tatsunori Hashimoto
- **Year/Venue:** 2025, EMNLP 2025 / ICLR 2025 Reasoning Workshop (Best Paper Award)
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.19393

**Background:** OpenAI's o1 demonstrated strong test-time scaling but kept methodology secret. DeepSeek-R1 succeeded but required millions of samples and multi-stage RL. Open replications were needed.

**Motivation:** What is the simplest possible approach to achieve test-time scaling and strong reasoning performance with minimal training data?

**Approach:** Two components: **(1) s1K dataset:** 1,000 carefully curated questions with reasoning traces distilled from Gemini Thinking Experimental, selected via three criteria (difficulty, diversity, quality). **(2) Budget forcing:** A simple decoding-time intervention with two operations — (a) enforce maximum: if the model exceeds a token limit, append the end-of-thinking delimiter and "Final Answer:" to force an answer; (b) enforce minimum: suppress the end-of-thinking delimiter and instead append "Wait" to the model's reasoning trace, making it continue thinking. The "Wait" string often triggers self-correction behavior. Budget forcing can be applied multiple times to scale thinking further. SFT on Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct with s1K (26 min on 16 H100s, ~$25 cost).

**Training Method:** Pure SFT (next-token prediction) on 1K samples. No RL, no MCTS.

**Key Results:** s1-32B exceeds o1-preview on competition math (up to 27% improvement on MATH and AIME24). Most sample-efficient reasoning model — matches models trained on 800K samples using only 1K. Budget forcing scales performance from 50% to 57% on AIME24. "Wait" is the most effective string for extrapolation.

**Weakness:** Budget forcing can be brittle — abrupt truncation may produce incomplete reasoning. Sequential scaling eventually hits context limits. SFT-only approach may not generalize as well as RL-trained models.

**Relevance to Our Work:** **High.** Budget forcing is the closest prior work to our cost-aware stopping mechanism. However, it uses hardcoded interventions (append "Wait" or truncate) rather than learned stopping. Our monitor agent would learn when to stop naturally rather than relying on fixed budgets.

### Boosting Accuracy and Efficiency of Budget Forcing via Reinforcement Learning

- **Authors:** Ravindra Aribowo Tarunokusumo, Rafael Fernandes Cunha
- **Year/Venue:** 2025, arXiv preprint
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.21398

**Background:** Budget forcing (from s1) relies solely on SFT, which on smaller models causes performance degradation due to verbose/unnatural responses. The SFT model often "overthinks" — generating excessive back-and-forth reasoning.

**Motivation:** Can RL improve the token efficiency and accuracy of budget forcing, especially for smaller models where SFT alone is suboptimal?

**Approach:** A two-stage framework: (1) SFT initialization with a modest reasoning dataset to teach the model the format of CoT with thinking delimiters, followed by (2) RL optimization to refine how the model uses its token budget. During inference, budget forcing dynamically controls thinking tokens. The RL phase uses GRPO-like group-relative optimization with accuracy reward. Key insight: RL recovers the losses from long-context SFT training (which causes small models to produce overly verbose responses) and significantly improves token efficiency.

**Training Method:** SFT + GRPO-based RL. Accuracy reward (binary). 1,500 training samples on a 1.5B model.

**Key Results:** SFT+RL achieves 14% higher accuracy than SFT-only across all compute budgets. Token usage reduced by over 40% compared to SFT-only model. Incomplete generations reduced by 52%. RL effectively eliminates verbose/overthinking behavior that SFT introduces.

**Weakness:** Only tested on 1.5B model and GSM8K. Single math dataset. Small training set may limit generalization.

**Relevance to Our Work:** **High.** Shows that RL can significantly improve token efficiency of budget-constrained reasoning. The finding that RL naturally reduces token usage while maintaining/increasing accuracy supports training a reward model that optimizes for both correctness and cost.

### Don't Overthink It: Preferring Shorter Thinking Chains for Improved LLM Reasoning

- **Authors:** Michael Hassid, Gabriel Synnaeve, Yossi Adi, Roy Schwartz
- **Year/Venue:** 2025, arXiv preprint (Meta FAIR / Hebrew University)
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17813

**Background:** Scaling test-time compute is the dominant strategy for improving reasoning LLMs, but it incurs massive computational costs and latency. The implicit assumption is that longer thinking = better reasoning.

**Motivation:** Does longer thinking actually lead to better reasoning, or are shorter chains more effective?

**Approach:** The authors first demonstrate that shorter reasoning chains are significantly more accurate — up to 34.5% more accurate than the longest chain for the same question. They then propose **short-m@k**, a novel inference method: execute k parallel generations, halt all generations as soon as the first m thinking processes finish. short-1@k takes the single shortest chain; short-3@k takes majority vote among the 3 shortest. The key insight is that faster-finishing chains tend to be more correct, so waiting for all generations is wasteful. The method reduces both tokens and wall-clock time. They also experiment with SFT on short vs. long chains.

**Training Method:** No training required — purely an inference method. Also experiments with SFT comparing training on short, long, and random chains.

**Key Results:** short-1@k (single shortest chain) matches or outperforms majority voting in low-compute regimes, with up to 40% fewer thinking tokens. short-3@k consistently surpasses majority voting at all compute budgets, with up to 33% wall-time reduction. Training on shorter reasoning chains yields better accuracy. Shortest chains are 50% shorter than random and 67% shorter than longest chains.

**Weakness:** Only tested on 3 math benchmarks; requires parallel inference infrastructure; m=1 may be insufficient for very hard problems.

**Relevance to Our Work:** **High.** Directly validates the hypothesis that shorter is often better in LLM reasoning. The early-stopping mechanism (halt when first m finish) is an inference-time version of our monitor's stopping function. Our agent could detect which generations are likely correct early using learned quality signals rather than just chain length.

### Over-Reasoning and Redundant Calculation of Large Language Models

- **Authors:** Cheng-Han Chiang, Hung-yi Lee
- **Year/Venue:** 2024, arXiv preprint
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.11467

**Background:** While CoT reasoning boosts LLM performance, it's unclear if LLMs know when to use CoT and whether the generated reasoning is always necessary. This was the first paper to systematically study CoT redundancy.

**Motivation:** Do LLMs generate redundant calculations and reasoning when they clearly don't need to?

**Approach:** The authors construct **GSM8K-Zero**, a math QA dataset where questions are intentionally trivial — they can be answered without any calculations (e.g., the answer is directly stated in the question or requires zero arithmetic). They test whether LLMs (Claude-2, Llama-2 variants, GPT-3.5, GPT-4) still generate lengthy CoT reasoning for these zero-calculation questions. They analyze why LLMs over-reason: (1) training data bias — models were fine-tuned on datasets where every question had reasoning steps; (2) instruction-tuning encourages verbose responses; (3) the model doesn't have metacognitive awareness to distinguish when reasoning is needed.

**Training Method:** No new training — evaluation of existing LLMs only.

**Key Results:** LLMs consistently over-reason on trivial problems — generating lengthy, unnecessary calculations for problems solvable in zero steps. Even GPT-4 generates redundant reasoning. The models appear to lack the ability to recognize when CoT is unnecessary.

**Weakness:** Small manually constructed dataset (GSM8K-Zero only); doesn't propose a solution; limited to math domain; dated models by current standards.

**Relevance to Our Work:** **High.** This is the earliest clear demonstration of the over-reasoning problem that our work addresses. The finding that LLMs lack metacognitive ability to know when reasoning is unnecessary directly motivates the need for an external monitor agent that can make this judgment.

### When More is Less: Understanding Chain-of-Thought Length in LLMs

- **Authors:** Yuyang Wu, Yifei Wang, Ziyu Ye, Tianqi Du, Stefanie Jegelka, Yisen Wang
- **Year/Venue:** 2025, arXiv preprint (Feb 2025, revised May 2025)
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.07266

**Background:** Conventional wisdom held that longer CoT = better reasoning. However, recent observations of "overthinking" (e.g., DeepSeek-R1 generating thousands of tokens for simple problems) challenge this assumption.

**Motivation:** Does reasoning performance consistently improve as CoTs grow longer, or is there an optimal length beyond which performance degrades?

**Approach:** A comprehensive investigation using three types of evidence: **(1) Controlled synthetic experiments:** 6-layer GPT-2 on arithmetic tasks at varying difficulty levels, manipulating CoT length directly. **(2) Real-world observations:** On production LLMs (DeepSeek-R1, Qwen, Llama). **(3) Theoretical analysis:** Proving the existence of an optimal CoT length. Core finding: task accuracy follows an inverted U-shaped curve with CoT length — performance initially improves as the model decomposes the problem, but eventually deteriorates as excessively long CoTs accumulate errors. Key theoretical result: optimal CoT length increases with task difficulty but decreases with model capability — more capable models naturally prefer shorter, more efficient reasoning (a "simplicity bias"). This bias is also observed during RL training, where models shift toward shorter CoTs as they become more accurate.

**Training Method:** Controlled experiments with GPT-2 from scratch; RL training analysis on reasoning models. Synthetic arithmetic datasets with controlled difficulty.

**Key Results:** Inverted U-shaped accuracy vs. CoT length confirmed across all settings. Optimal length scales with difficulty (2-digit multiplication: ~4 steps; 4-digit: ~8 steps). More capable models prefer shorter CoTs. During RL training, CoT length naturally decreases as accuracy improves. Fine-tuning on shorter chains yields 12-18% absolute accuracy improvement over longer chains.

**Weakness:** Synthetic experiments use toy models; real-world observations are correlational; only arithmetic and math tasks studied.

**Relevance to Our Work:** **Highest.** This paper provides the strongest theoretical and empirical evidence for the existence of an optimal reasoning length and the diminishing returns of excessive computation. This directly motivates our monitor agent: if longer isn't always better, a learned stopping mechanism can find the sweet spot per instance. The "simplicity bias" finding also suggests our monitor could learn to be more aggressive with stopping for stronger base models.
